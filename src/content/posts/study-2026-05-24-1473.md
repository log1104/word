# Planted with Purpose: A Study of Luke 13:6

**You:** Study Luke 13:6 - 9

**AI:** In Luke 13:6–9, Jesus provides a parable that serves as a sobering postscript to His preceding warning: “Unless you repent, you will all likewise perish.” This parable illustrates the relationship between God’s patience and His righteous demand for fruitfulness in the lives of His people.

### The Context of Expectation (Verse 6)
Jesus describes a man who had a fig tree ([Strong's G4808](https://biblehub.com/strongs/greek/4808.htm)) planted in his vineyard. In the Old Testament, the fig tree and the vineyard were common symbols for the nation of Israel ([Isaiah 5:1–7](https://bible-xref.247base.uk/Isaiah/5/1); [Hosea 9:10](https://bible-xref.247base.uk/Hosea/9/10)). 

The owner “came looking for fruit ([Strong's G2590](https://biblehub.com/strongs/greek/2590.htm)) on it and found none.” This mirrors the expectation found in [Matthew 3:8](https://bible-xref.247base.uk/Matthew/3/8), where John the Baptist demands "fruit in keeping with repentance." God does not merely look for religious profession; He looks for the actual evidence of a transformed life.

### The Verdict of Justice (Verse 7)
The owner’s patience was not a sign of indifference. He had waited three years—a period sufficient for a healthy tree to produce. His command to the vinedresser is blunt: “Cut it down! ([Strong's G1581](https://biblehub.com/strongs/greek/1581.htm)). Why should it even waste ([Strong's G2673](https://biblehub.com/strongs/greek/2673.htm)) the ground?”

The word for "waste" or "cumber" is *katargei*, meaning to make idle, useless, or to drain the resources of the soil without giving anything back. This warns us that a life without repentance is not merely "neutral"—it is a drain on the grace and resources God provides. As seen in [John 15:2](https://bible-xref.247base.uk/John/15/2), every branch that does not bear fruit is taken away.

### The Intercession of Grace (Verse 8)
The vinedresser (representing Christ’s intercessory role) pleads for a stay of execution: “Sir, leave it alone for one more year, until I dig around it and fertilize it.”

This "digging around and fertilizing" represents the active, sometimes painful, and intensive work of God’s grace to provoke a response in the heart. This aligns with [2 Peter 3:9](https://bible-xref.247base.uk/2%20Peter/3/9), which explains that God is patient, "not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance."

### The Finality of Judgment (Verse 9)
The parable ends with a conditional future: “If it bears fruit next year, fine; but if not, you can cut it down.” 

Jesus does not finish the story by telling us if the tree eventually bore fruit. He leaves the ending open because the listeners—and we—are the ones who must provide the conclusion. The primary truths here are:
1.  **Judgment is certain:** The tree is not granted an infinite amount of time.
2.  **Grace is temporary:** The "one more year" is a period of mercy intended to lead to action, not to justify further delay.
3.  **Fruit is the requirement:** There is no third option between bearing fruit and being cut down.

As [Hebrews 6:7–8](https://bible-xref.247base.uk/Hebrews/6/7) describes, land that drinks in the rain but produces only thorns is "worthless and near to being cursed; its end is to be burned." This parable is a call to examine whether the "cultivation" we receive from God is resulting in the fruit He rightfully expects.

**AI:** To understand the weight of this parable, we should look closely at the word used for the tree being "planted." This word tells us a great deal about the relationship between the owner and the tree.

### 1. The Original Word
The Greek word is **φυτεύω** (*phyteuō*).
**Pronunciation:** foo-tyoo'-o
**Strong's Number:** [Strong's G5452](https://biblehub.com/strongs/greek/5452.htm)

### 2. What It Can Mean
In the New Testament, *phyteuō* is almost always translated as "plant." Whether it’s a farmer planting a literal seed or a person establishing a garden, the core idea is **purposeful placement**. It describes the act of taking something and intentionally setting it into the ground so that it can take root and grow. It isn't an accident; it's a deliberate investment of time and space.

### 3. The Grammar Details
The morphology for this word in Luke 13:6 is **V-RPM/P-AFS**. Let’s break that down into plain English:

*   **V (Verb):** This is an action word.
*   **R (Perfect):** This is the most important part! In Greek, the "Perfect" tense describes an action that was completed in the past but has **ongoing results** in the present. This tree wasn't just tossed there; it was established and remains in its state of being "the planted one."
*   **P (Participle):** It’s functioning like a description or a title here—"the having-been-planted tree."
*   **M/P (Middle/Passive):** The tree is the one *receiving* the action. The tree did not plant itself; someone else put it there.
*   **AFS (Accusative Feminine Singular):** These are grammatical markers that help the word "planted" lock onto the word "fig tree" so the reader knows exactly which object is being described.

### 4. Where Else It Appears
You can see the depth of this word by how it is used in other famous passages:

*   **[Matthew 15:13](https://bible-xref.247base.uk/Matthew/15/13):** Jesus says, "Every plant that My heavenly Father has not **planted** (*phyteuō*) will be pulled up by the roots." This shows that God is the Master Gardener who decides what truly belongs in His kingdom.
*   **[1 Corinthians 3:6](https://bible-xref.247base.uk/1%20Corinthians/3/6):** Paul writes, "I **planted** (*phyteuō*), Apollos watered, but God gave the growth." Here, it describes the hard work of starting a church or sharing the Gospel for the first time.

By looking at these, we see that *phyteuō* is about **ownership and origin**. If something is "planted," someone is responsible for it and expects it to grow.

### 5. Why This Matters
In English, "that was planted" can sound like a simple piece of background information. But the original Greek highlights a critical truth: **The fig tree was there by design.**

Because it was "planted" (Perfect tense) in a "vineyard," it was given special status. It wasn't a wild tree growing on a random hillside; it was intentionally placed in a protected, cultivated environment. This changes how we view the "lack of fruit." The owner's anger isn't just because the tree is empty, but because the tree has received the *benefit* of being planted in his vineyard without giving anything back. 

It reminds us that if we have been "planted" in the reach of God's Word and grace, we are under a specific expectation to produce what we were designed for. We aren't just "there"; we are "planted" with a purpose.
